# AI Usage Statement
As would be expected from the topic of this syllabus, I used AI to help me conceive and plan. ChatGPT and Claude provided me with a lot of helpful sources and in some cases helped me to augment my thinking. For example, before my results from ChatGPT, I had not heard of Katherine Crawford's *Atlas of AI* and I consider this one of the most important texts in the syllabus. Other informative works that ChatGPT made me aware of include *Graphesis* by Johanna Drucker and *A Geology of Media* by Jussi Parikka. ChatGPT included works that I thought essential to addressing ethics and philosophy regarding writing history with AI, such as Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* and Donna Haraway's *Cyborg Manifesto*.

While I had some initial ideas for how AI chatbots could be used to produce experimental methodologies for historical narratives, the ideas that ChatGPT put together helped me to further articulate these ideas, particularly in the speculative methodologies section of the chat. 

I think I got such good results from ChatGPT because I started the chat by asking about historical methodology in general, narrowed to the subfield of medieval to confirm it was providing accurate answers and context, and then moved on to the deeper questions on feminist historiography and finally historiography informed by AI. 

Below is my prompt for experimental methodologies using AI chatbots. This prompt appeared in the middle of a multi-shot prompting conversation with ChatGPT, Version 5, on October 26, 2025.

> At this point, I would like you to review everything in this chat string and then make reasoned but highly creative speculations about what new methodological approaches might emerge in the next twenty years. Please mark these answers according to how experimental or creative you are being. I'm not interested as much in likelihood as I am in seeing a large range in what might happen next. For example, how would AI approach methodology in ways that are dramatically different from the types of methodologies that have emerged in the last century? ## A Case Study for Writing History with AI 
This case study's topic is the creation of Columbus Day as a national holiday, and is an exercise in analyzing historical thinking as a baseline for what AI chatbots can do for writing history and for becoming aware of filter bubbles.[^presentation] The students will replicate this exercise on Day 2 of Week 2.

[^presentation]:I shared this case study in my PhD Public Presentation on November 3, 2025.

Sam Wineburg is one of the most well-respected writers in history education. He has spent the last thirty years investigating how students, as well as the general public, construct their understandings of historical events. You can see the origins of my thinking on the importance of iterative questioning from this quote from Wineburg: “True historical inquiry must end where it begins: with a question mark.”[^wineburg1] 

[^wineburg1]:Sam Wineburg, *Why Learn History When It’s Already On Your Phone?*, p.81

In Chapter 4 of his 2018 book, *Why Learn History When It’s Already on Your Phone?*, Wineburg gives an exercise to Jacob, a successful, high achieving AP US History student at a Bay Area high school, and then gives the same exercise to a group of history graduate students specializing in fields other than US History. He provides them only with a copy of a short *New York Times* article from July 22, 1892. He tells them simply to provide a historical analysis of the document. Jacob talks cogently about what Columbus did and whether it’s justified to honor him with a federal holiday. The graduate students, instead, immediately started to wonder about the context of late nineteenth century US politics, bringing up demographic trends and the rise of nativism. The graduate students spent no time evaluating Columbus’s actions and instead kept their analysis on the political pressures that influenced the decision-making process of President Benjamin Harrison. Jacob issued pronouncements, reaching a conclusion about Columbus, and the graduate students raised questions, puzzling about late nineteenth century politics and society. 

When I encounter a new source, like the graduate students in Wineburg’s exercise, I also return to question formulation and focus on the context from the time of the document’s creation. But would AI do this? In order to find out, I obtained the same 1892 article and uploaded it into Chat GPT along with the prompt, 'Please write a historical analysis of this article.' Chat GPT provided a three page analysis that included sentences about the 1890s as 'marked by intense debates over immigration and national identity. Southern and Eastern European immigrants, Catholic and Jewish communities, and labor movements were transforming the urban landscape.' No puzzlement, but it did sound to me like high quality historical thinking on par with the contextual focus that characterized the graduate students’ responses. 
[ChatGPT analysis of 1892 proclamation, ChatGPT Version 5, logged in as Jonathan Seyfried, October 22, 2025 →](pdfs/chat-gpt-logged-in-columbus-day.pdf)

I then wondered if my earlier work on Chat GPT had influenced this answer, since I had been using it for many other historical inquiries to prepare for this presentation. I opened a browser app that I hadn’t used before, and in an incognito window I went to Chat GPT. With the same exact prompt, Chat GPT returned different results. When responding to an anonymous user without an account, Chat GPT also did better than the AP US History student, in that it did address the historical context of immigration in the late nineteenth century rather than only evaluating Columbus himself. Unlike the answer provided to my account, this time Chat GPT included two paragraphs on the moral dimension of honoring Columbus. It also left out Catholics and lacked any critique of assimilation as a cultural script. At work here is what’s known as a filter bubble. 
[ChatGPT analysis of 1892 proclamation, ChatGPT Version 5, no log in, October 22, 2025 →](pdfs/chat-gpt-no-log-in-columbus-day.pdf)

A filter bubble is the term for when a user’s experience with digital content is different from others based on information gathered about them. When encountering this type of behavior in search results, there could be one of three things happening.[^gustavsen] The user might have input customizations for their account, as Chat GPT allows for that in its settings. Another filtering mechanism is the guardrails put into the system, designed to avoid scenarios in which the chatbot might encourage someone to do harm to themselves or others. This is carried out by human labor to train the social harm out of a newly created AI Chatbot. For example, Chat GPT is trained by hundreds of low-paid workers in Kenya to rate certain answers as off-limits.[^mollick] The third type of filter is algorithmic, meaning that the output is different based on factors that the system perceives about the user during the interaction. For example, if you search Google for best hospitals, you get different results if you are doing that search in Albuquerque or London because Google uses your location data to adjust the results it gives you. 

[^gustavsen]:UNM’s Humanities Librarian David Gustavsen helped me to understand how to define and analyze filter bubbles

[^mollick]:Ethan Mollick, *Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI*, p.14

In the situation here, for the Columbus Day document, ChatGPT provided me with a more complex answer based on its experience with my logged-in account. It is true that people, in a general similarity to ChatGPT, adjust what they say based on their audience. However, when considering my abilities versus abilities of an AI chatbot, I am not trying to argue that I am free of bias or that I will never change what I say based on my audience. Rather, this filter bubble experience points to something important about the iterative quality of what I do when I encounter sources. Nothing is inherently off-limits in my thoughts. To some, this might seem like a marginal problem, but much of what drives our inquiry as historians is the hope of shining a spotlight on how a particular social context influenced the guardrails for the behavior of historical subjects in past societies, and exploring what counted as a violation of norms. So, even as they might at times present satisfying historical context, AI chatbots can soften exactly the rough edges whose roughness we find most important to examine.
